How to play
  • Bring your rival's core from 20 to 0. Each player draws 1 card a turn and gains 1 more ink per turn, up to 10.
  • Spend ink to play cards from your hand. Units stay on your side (5 at most). Spells resolve and are gone.
  • Units can attack once per turn, starting the turn after they're played. Attacker and defender damage each other at the same time.
  • If a player has no cards left to draw, they take growing fatigue damage instead.
  • Click a card to play it, or click one of your units and then a target to attack. Press Escape to cancel.

      Training stages

        How the rival works

        It only knows what you'd know

        The rival never reads your hand or the order of either deck. It sees what a player at the table sees: the board, discard piles, card counts, its own hand, and every card you've revealed. Before deciding, it builds several guessed worlds. Each one fills your hidden hand from deck lists that fit the cards you've shown, so it reasons from facts it has observed rather than assumptions about you.

        It looks ahead

        Novice picks the move that looks best right now, with some noise. Adept plays out the rest of its turn for each option. Master plans its turn with beam search, checks your likely reply in four guessed worlds, and scores boards with its trained specialist evaluator.

        One lesson matches the write-up. Deeper search alone added nothing: with the evaluator held fixed, Master's search beat Adept's only half the time. The gain came from what self-play taught the evaluator.

        It learned what a good board looks like

        Every option is scored by an evaluator that weighs ten board features: core difference, power, guard, unit count, cards in hand, ink, walls, shields, lethal pressure and cards left in deck. Novice and Adept use hand-set weights. Master uses weights learned from self-play, a separate set for each deck.

        Trained in stages

        The training follows the staged recipe from the first-place write-up of a Kaggle card-battle AI competition:

        1. Broad self-play over every deck.
        2. A single-deck specialist per deck, trained against a frozen ladder of opponents.
        3. A round-robin arena that keeps the strongest version of each deck and shows the matchups between them.

        Identical copies of a card count as one option, which mirrors how that write-up merged equivalent options. Their agent learned its policy with a Transformer trained by PPO. This small browser game uses an evolved evaluator plus search, so it runs instantly on your device.
